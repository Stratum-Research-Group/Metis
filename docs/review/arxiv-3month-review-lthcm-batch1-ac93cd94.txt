LTHCM - TOP-PAPER TAKEAWAYS, 3 AUG - 3 NOV 2026 (BATCH 1 OF THE THREE-MONTH REVIEW)
Two papers worth your time for LTHCM out of the 42,755-paper pool. Why these two: both attack
the exact question LTHCM answers (how an assistant remembers over long horizons), one from the
weight side, one from the external-store side, and both have real numbers, not just claims.

====================================================================
1. Continual Learning Mechanisms Compose for Long-Horizon Memorization
arXiv:2609.06986 (7 Sep 2026), Zhang, Zhang, Khashabi, Shu - Johns Hopkins
Attention: #2 Hugging Face daily paper of 16 Sep (372 upvotes)
Coverage: full HTML text read (method, protocol, main results).

WHAT IT IS
A study of whether a language model can memorize information that arrives over time by
updating its weights, without keeping any raw earlier data and without a task label at test
time. They call this long-horizon memorization: 100 small query-answer tasks learned one
after another by continual fine-tuning. They built three 100-task datasets (arbitrary symbol
associations, LLM-generated fictional facts, natural questions filtered from public QA) and
systematically composed known continual-learning mechanisms: a data anchor (generative
replay), a function anchor (self-distillation), a weight anchor (importance regularization,
EWC/SI family), crossed with two LoRA allocation rules (shared vs merged LoRA).

WHAT IT ACTUALLY SHOWED (their numbers)
- Naive sequential fine-tuning: 1.2% average final retention after 100 tasks.
- Best single mechanism alone: 8.1%.
- Best composition (all three anchors + merged LoRA): 34.9% average final retention, top-3 on
  all three datasets, 28x over naive.
- The data anchor (replay) and merged LoRA are the two biggest gains and interact
  super-additively on all three datasets (measured with a proper factorial experiment, not
  vibes). Merged LoRA = fold each task's adapter into the dense weights, start a fresh one.
- Method note: they searched the composition space with task-level successive halving before
  the factorial run - a cheap screen then a careful confirm, same discipline as your
  text-vs-graph protocol preference.

LIMITS
- This is memorization of associations, not reasoning or generalization.
- Even the best composition answers only about a third of previously learned queries correctly
  after 100 tasks (34.9% average final retention, measured as accuracy over all learned tasks
  after the last update). Weight-space memory at a 100-task horizon is lossy, full stop.
- 100 tasks is the longest horizon tested; nothing says the curve holds at 1,000.
- 100 tasks here means 100 small query-answer task sets, not 100 individual facts.
- Scale (PDF-verified): base model Qwen3-4B-Base. Symbol-QA: 10,000 random key-value
  associations (100 per task). LLM-QA: 10,000 generated pairs across 100 fictional topics
  (100 per task). Real-QA: 5,000 natural QA pairs filtered so the base model cannot already
  answer them (50 per task). So this is a 4B model memorizing thousands of small associations,
  not frontier-scale facts.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR LTHCM (my judgment, not the paper's)
- Consistent with LTHCM's core design choice (external, inspectable memory instead of baking
  knowledge into weights): the strongest weight-side method in a careful study still recovers
  only about a third of learned associations after 100 sequential updates. But note the paper
  does not test retrieval or external memory stores - it says they exist and sets them aside -
  so this is not comparative evidence that an external store wins. It only bounds what
  weight-only memory can do.
- The continual-update recipe (merged LoRA + generative replay + self-distillation +
  importance regularization) and the 100-task retention datasets are Paideia and Dokime
  material; they are carried in those project files, not here.

2. Caching for the Future: Scrub Jay Episodic Memory Principles for Agent Memory Systems
arXiv:2608.04746 (5 Aug 2026), Bhandari, Wadhwani, Kumar, Narang - BITS Pilani
Coverage: full HTML text read (architecture, mechanisms, headline results).

WHAT IT IS
An agent memory architecture (ScrubJay-MEM) copied from how scrub jays cache food: each
memory is stored as a What-Where-When tuple plus a perishability coefficient pi and a utility
horizon tau, auto-classified at write time (LLM prompt, keyword fallback). Retrieval scores
combine content similarity, context similarity, temporal utility, and a graph bonus with
query-adaptive weights; decay is exponential per memory with type-conditioned rates (stable
facts decay slowly, ephemeral details fast). A prospective buffer preloads anticipated
memories; a retroactive step revises decay parameters when new information arrives at O(1)
LLM calls per update.

WHAT IT ACTUALLY SHOWED (their numbers)
- On their new Temporal Generalization Test (TGT: 20 instances, 96 memories each across four
  perishability classes, 66 queries per instance at five retention intervals: I1 (0-1
  sessions), I2 (3-5, seen), I3 (8-12, UNSEEN), I4 (20-25, seen), I5 (40-60, unseen);
  1,320 queries total; single seed; qwen3:30b generation; llama3.2:3b judge, chosen
  cross-family to avoid self-preference).
- Their metric (Eq. 16, verified against the rendered PDF equation image, so the
  transcription is certain): GenGap = Acc(I2) + Acc(I4) - 2*Acc(I3).
- SIGN INCONSISTENCY IN THE PAPER - flagged, not resolved. With this formula, positive
  GenGap means accuracy at the unseen interval I3 is BELOW the mean of the two seen-interval
  accuracies (GenGap = 2 x (chord - Acc(I3))). The paper's prose disagrees with itself:
  the definition paragraph says it "measures how far the system's accuracy at the unseen
  interpolation interval falls below the linear interpolation" (consistent with the formula:
  positive = falls below) but then says "Positive GenGap indicates robust generalization -
  accuracy at the unseen interval at or above the seen-interval baseline" (contradicts the
  formula), and Finding 1 says the baselines' "negative GenGap" means their I3 accuracy is
  "below the linear interpolation" (backwards from the formula). The stated formula and the
  quoted interpretation disagree in sign. With this formula, positive means unseen accuracy
  below mean seen accuracy. I cannot treat its sign as evidence of good generalization
  without resolving that inconsistency.
- Author-reported scores (no sign interpretation attached): ScrubJay-MEM +0.108; flat-
  retrieval baselines negative (hybrid RRF -0.054, no-temporal-model -0.022, flat-recency
  -0.046); LLM-only control +0.181, which the paper itself discounts as an artifact of
  near-random answering having no systematic per-interval bias. The decay ablation is
  reported as collapsing GenGap from +0.108 to +0.019 (5.7x) while staleness accuracy stays
  at 82.3% - reported here as the authors' numbers; under the contradictory sign definition
  I do not infer a direction of improvement from it.
- What survives independently of the GenGap question: on MemoryAgentBench EventQA-64k (a
  separate, pre-existing benchmark, not theirs), ScrubJay-MEM improves F1 over the compared
  baselines (their Table 1), and the portable design idea - type-conditioned perishability
  decay over stored memories - does not depend on TGT's metric definition.
- Benchmark scale caveats: 20 instances and 1,320 queries is small; TGT data was
  LLM-generated (qwen3:30b) with a 3B judge; single seed. Real within this benchmark, not
  independently validated.
- On MemoryAgentBench EventQA-64k under a llama3.1:8b backbone: 61.58 F1, +2.66 over Mem0,
  +3.09 over Qwen3-Embedding-4B, best of the systems compared.
- Their own contextual caveats: the LLM-only control's +0.181 GenGap is discussed by them
  as a low-accuracy artifact; a RAG-temporal variant beats their system on combined accuracy
  at the cost of one LLM call per query; TGT data was LLM-generated (qwen3:30b) with a
  llama3.2:3b cross-family judge.

LIMITS (several are theirs, stated plainly in the paper)
- Gains narrow under stronger backbones and reverse on fact-consolidation tasks. The win is
  scoped to temporal reasoning over perishable facts, not general memory quality.
- The evaluated v1 substitutes parameter-free surrogates at three points where the blueprint
  describes trained components: fixed fusion weights, an LLM-prompt perishability classifier
  with keyword fallback, and heuristic keyword-triggered query weights. The biology framing is
  nice; the shipped mechanism is engineering heuristics.
- TGT is their own benchmark, not independently replicated - and its headline metric has
  the unresolved internal sign inconsistency flagged above. Treat the TGT/GenGap claims as
  unestablished until the authors clarify which convention Eq. 16 intends.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR LTHCM (my judgment)
- The single most portable idea: per-memory, type-conditioned temporal decay as a first-class
  field - "meeting room today" vs "Nick's profession". Grounding against current code: the
  Metis repo review delivered today (HEAD 88d9166) found LTHCM ships a default-on causality
  filter, but that is a "not from the future" timestamp check on candidates, not
  perishability-aware scoring of stored items by content type. So the proposal gap is real
  against that reviewed source; I did not re-read the retrieval code fresh for this file.
- The TGT/GenGap eval construction is Dokime material and is carried in that file.
- Do not import the architecture. The transferable parts are the perishability field and the
  query-adaptive retrieval weighting; whether their hypergraph adds anything over LTHCM's
  existing structure is untested and I am not claiming it.
